The plan

How Oscar is built, in what order, and what my daughter does in each mission. Dates are targets at a weekend pace.

Why this robot

I want my daughter to grow up treating AI as something you can open, teach and correct. A chat window does not do that for a small child. A robot that follows her around the room and gets things wrong in front of her does.

The body is chillibasket's open-source Wall-E replica: about 310 printed parts, two drive motors, seven servos, an Arduino and a Raspberry Pi. The stock build is remote controlled. This project adds three AI layers on top.

  1. It has to survive a child. Tracks cannot fall over. A walking robot falls, and servos break when it does.
  2. Every session ends with something that works. Each mission below lists my part, hers and the one idea she should take away. If a mission has nothing for her to do, the mission is wrong.
  3. She has to be able to teach it. The main lesson is a game: she shows the robot her toys, names them, and watches it get better or worse depending on the examples she gives.

Why there is no reinforcement learning on this robot

My first idea was to copy what Pollen Robotics' Microduck does: train a policy in a physics simulator, then run it on the real robot. That method earns its keep on walking and balance. A tracked robot is stable by construction, so a learned drive policy would have nothing hard to learn.

It stays in the plan as a side quest. Microduck's simulator and training code are open source, so we can watch a simulated duck fall over many times and learn to walk on my laptop.

How it fits together

Three computers, each doing what it is good at. The Arduino keeps the motion smooth, the Pi handles senses and anything that must stay private, and a bigger machine does the heavy thinking.

In the body

Arduino Uno

  • Two 12 V drive motors through a motor shield
  • Seven servos through an I2C servo driver: head rotation, neck top, neck bottom, two eyes, two arms
  • Animation queue and smooth servo acceleration
  • Two distance sensors that stop it at walls and edges
  • Battery level monitor
⇄USB serial
115200 baud
In the body

Raspberry Pi 5

  • Camera behind a flat window in one eye, microphone, speaker
  • Bluetooth game controller, paired with the Pi directly
  • Stock web interface: joystick, sounds, block editor
  • Face following, wake word and speech to text, on the device
  • Teach game: pictures, classifier, answers
⇄Home Wi-Fi
text only
Off the robot

Laptop or cloud

  • Speech to text, only if the Pi proves too slow
  • Language model that answers and calls robot tools
  • Reinforcement learning side quest in simulation

Power: one 12 V battery feeds the motors directly and the Pi through a 12 V to 5 V converter. Which battery is still open. The current direction is a Bosch 12 V tool pack behind a rear flap, see open decisions.

Missions

Each mission is one or two weekends and ends with something that works. The printer runs between our sessions, so a session never consists of waiting. The eyes and head come first: they carry the character, they are small prints, and they need no battery. The body panels come last. Dates are targets at a weekend pace.

Why not print everything first

The first version of this plan followed the engineering order: print about 310 parts, paint them, then assemble and test unit by unit. The original builder needed more than a month of near-daily printing. That is a month in which nothing moves, and a small child will not wait for it.

The new order costs me rework. Head, eyes and electronics are wired on the desk first and wired again inside the body. I accept that.

Software

The plan is to keep the stock code running and add the AI as a separate service next to it, so the robot still works as a remote-controlled toy when the clever parts are broken.

Motion
Stock Arduino sketch from chillibasket/walle-replica. It queues animations, smooths servo acceleration and reads commands from the Pi without blocking. New: two distance sensors on the Arduino stop forward motion at an obstacle or a drop. The check runs on the Arduino, so it holds even when the Pi has crashed. Commands are single characters at 115200 baud: w a s d drive, q stops, j l tilt the head, i k move the eyes.
Remote control
New: a Bluetooth game controller is the main control. It pairs with the Pi, and a small service turns sticks and buttons into the commands above. No phone, browser or Wi-Fi is involved. If the connection drops, the service sends stop. Stock Flask web interface on the Pi, kept for setup and debugging: on-screen joystick, manual servo control, sounds, text to speech, a drag-and-drop block editor and a camera stream. A systemd service starts it at boot.
Face following
New. A face detector on the Pi turns the offset of the face from the image centre into head and eye commands. Candidates are MediaPipe and OpenCV.
Voice loop
New. Wake word or a push-to-talk button on the controller, speech to text, language model with tool calls, text to speech. Candidates are openWakeWord, a Whisper-family model and Piper. Which language model runs behind it is an open decision. The model never sends raw serial commands. It calls four named tools with limits on speed and duration, and the service translates those into the commands above.
Teach game
New. Image embeddings from a small pretrained vision model plus a nearest-neighbour classifier, all on the Pi. Her examples are stored as pictures in folders named after each object, so she can look at exactly what the robot learned from.

The stock code is licensed GPL-3.0. Everything I derive from it will be published under the same licence, in the project repository, which already holds the source of this site. Our own new code will be published there too.

Safety and privacy

This is a robot with a lithium battery, a camera and a microphone in a house with a small child. These rules are part of the design.

  • Battery. Whatever battery we pick has a hard case and is charged outside the robot. A fuse and a low-voltage cutoff sit between the battery and everything else.
  • Walls and edges. Two distance sensors stop forward motion at an obstacle or a drop, whatever the controller, the Pi or the language model asked for.
  • Lost connection. When the game controller disconnects or its battery dies, the robot stops. The Arduino should also stop by itself when the Pi goes silent. That second check needs a heartbeat the stock code does not have, and it is not written yet.
  • Pinch points. Treads and arm linkages can catch small fingers. Drive speed is capped in software while she is the driver.
  • Printer and paint. The nozzle and bed are hot, so she watches and I remove the parts. Spray painting is an adult job, outdoors.
  • No pictures or audio of her leave the house. Face following and the teach game run on the robot. Speech is transcribed at home, and only the text goes to the language model.
  • The microphone has a physical off switch. Nothing is recorded before the wake word or the talk button.
  • This site. It does not give her name or age and shows no photo or recording of her. Photos show the robot and its parts, and location data is removed before a photo is published.
  • Home network only. The stock web interface has a simple login that is not meant for untrusted networks, so the robot is never exposed to the internet.

Open decisions

  • Battery. The current direction is a Bosch Professional 12 V tool pack in a printed sleeve behind a flap in the back panel. I saw exactly that in another builder's robot, and the pack pulls out to the rear. It is not final: I do not like a tool battery in a toy, and Bosch makes these packs for its own tools only. The alternatives I looked at are a USB-C power bank with a trigger board, a small camera battery, and the DJI RoboMaster battery. The test is the same for all of them: easy to swap, safe for her to handle, simple to wire.
  • Power button. A button on the front panel, as on the other builder's robot. A plain latching switch in the battery line would cut the Pi without a clean shutdown. A momentary button on a small power switch board lets the Pi shut down first. That choice waits for the battery.
  • Language model. A cloud model answers faster and better. A local model on the laptop keeps even the text at home. I will measure response time for both before choosing.
  • Sharing the serial port. The stock web interface owns the connection to the Arduino. The AI service and the controller service should send their commands through that interface instead of opening the port a second time. How cleanly that works is untested.
  • Camera fit. The camera eye gets a flat clear window instead of a glass lens, and the standard Camera Module 3 lens clears the opening if it sits within about 20 mm of it. Where the camera sits on the printed mount is not measured yet.
  • Sensor bracket. Where the two distance sensors mount on the front without spoiling the look, and whether the extra code still fits in the Arduino Uno's memory.
  • Pace. One session a week is a guess. I will adjust after mission 3.
  • Wake word or talk button. A wake word has to cope with a child's voice. A talk button is simpler and only listens while it is held. Both are planned, and she decides which one she uses.

Later upgrades

  • Driven arms and hands. The other builder's robot moves its arms at more joints than ours will. That needs new arm parts, more motors and more code, so it comes after mission 13.
  • A phone in the controller. A game controller with a phone clamped in it, showing the camera picture and the web page. Maybe.
  • Not planned: screens in the eyes. Some builds replace the lenses with small displays. Oscar keeps his glass eye and his camera eye.

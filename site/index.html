A Wall-E replica my daughter can teach

I am building a 3D-printed, tank-tracked robot with my daughter and giving it a brain she can talk to, argue with and train herself. This page is the plan. I will update it as the build moves, including the parts that go wrong.

Why this robot

I want my daughter to grow up treating AI as something you can open, teach and correct. A chat window does not do that for a small child. A robot that follows her around the room and gets things wrong in front of her does.

The body is chillibasket's open-source Wall-E replica: about 310 printed parts, two drive motors, seven servos, an Arduino and a Raspberry Pi. The stock build is remote controlled. This project adds three AI layers on top.

  1. It has to survive a child. Tracks cannot fall over. A walking robot falls, and servos break when it does.
  2. She has a job in every phase. Each phase below lists my part and hers. If a phase has nothing for her to do, the phase is wrong.
  3. She has to be able to teach it. The main lesson is a game: she shows the robot her toys, names them, and watches it get better or worse depending on the examples she gives.

Why there is no reinforcement learning on this robot

My first idea was to copy what Pollen Robotics' Microduck does: train a policy in a physics simulator, then run it on the real robot. That method earns its keep on walking and balance. A tracked robot is stable by construction, so a learned drive policy would have nothing hard to learn.

It stays in the plan as a side quest. Microduck's simulator and training code are open source, so we can watch a simulated duck fall over many times and learn to walk on my laptop.

How it fits together

Three computers, each doing what it is good at. The Arduino keeps the motion smooth, the Pi handles senses and anything that must stay private, and a bigger machine does the heavy thinking.

In the body

Arduino Uno

  • Two 12 V drive motors through a motor shield
  • Seven servos through an I2C servo driver: head rotation, neck top, neck bottom, two eyes, two arms
  • Animation queue and smooth servo acceleration
  • Two distance sensors that stop it at walls and edges
  • Battery level monitor
⇄USB serial
115200 baud
In the body

Raspberry Pi 5

  • Camera behind one eye, microphone, speaker
  • Bluetooth game controller, paired with the Pi directly
  • Stock web interface: joystick, sounds, block editor
  • Face following, wake word and speech to text, on the device
  • Teach game: pictures, classifier, answers
⇄Home Wi-Fi
text only
Off the robot

Laptop or cloud

  • Speech to text, only if the Pi proves too slow
  • Language model that answers and calls robot tools
  • Reinforcement learning side quest in simulation

Power: one 12 V tool battery feeds the motors directly and the Pi through a 12 V to 5 V buck converter.

    Drive base and remote control

    Jan 2027Not started
    Build
    Assemble the treads and both geared motors. Wire the Arduino, motor shield, battery holder, fuse, low-voltage cutoff and buck converter. Fit the two distance sensors and make the Arduino stop at a wall or a drop. Flash the stock sketch and install the stock web interface on the Pi.     First drive, with the game controller in her hands. Then try to drive it into a wall and off a low step, and find out that it refuses.

  6. 5

    AI layer one: it looks at you

    Mar 2027Not started
    Build
    Face detection on the Pi steers the head rotation and eye servos to keep a face centred. Everything runs on the robot and no image leaves it.
    Her part
    Hide and seek. Find out where it loses her: backlight, hats, distance, turning away.
    Lesson
    Machine vision fails in specific, repeatable ways, and you can find them by experiment.
    Done when

  7. 6

    AI layer two: it listens and answers

    Mar to Apr 2027Not started
    Build
    Wake word and speech to text on the Pi, with the laptop as fallback if the Pi is too slow, then a language model that can answer and call four robot tools: drive, look, play_animation and play_sound. Text to speech on the Pi. A button on the game controller works as push-to-talk next to the wake word. Target: the answer starts within two seconds, because a small child will not wait longer.
    Her part
    Decide the robot's character and its rules, in her own words. We write them into its instructions together and test whether it keeps them.
    Lesson
    You steer this kind of machine with language, and it does not always do what you meant.
    Done when
    She asks it to come to her, and it does.
  8. 7

    AI layer three: she teaches it

    Apr to May 2027Not started
    Build
    Teach mode. She holds up an object and says its name, and the robot takes about twenty pictures. A small pretrained vision model turns each picture into a list of numbers, and a nearest-neighbour classifier compares new pictures against her examples. Retraining takes seconds. The robot says what it thinks it sees and how sure it is.
    Her part
    Teach it her toys. Then try to fool it. Then fix it with better examples.
    Lesson
    It learns from examples, varied examples work better, and it can be sure and wrong at once.
    Done when
    It names five of her toys correctly and she has fooled it at least once.

Why the Pi went from 4 GB to 8 GB

Version 1 of this plan chose the 4 GB board because US list prices put the 8 GB board at about double. Swiss shop prices are much closer: CHF 129.00 for 4 GB at Bastelgarage against CHF 164.90 for 8 GB at pi-shop.ch.

Everything this plan runs on the Pi fits in 4 GB. The extra CHF 36 buys room to run speech to text on the robot itself, so it keeps working when my laptop is closed. More memory does not make the Pi fast enough to host the language model. That stays off the robot.

Why a power-tool battery

The original design uses a bare three-cell LiPo pouch that needs a balance charger and a fire bag. In a house with a small child I want a hard case and a charger that is a dock. A Bosch Professional 12 V pack also has three lithium-ion cells, so the voltage matches. Two packs and the fast charger cost CHF 87.90, about CHF 18 more than the LiPo, its charger and the bag.

Two caveats. Bosch makes these packs for its own tools only, so using one in a robot is at my own risk. And the robot has to bring its own fuse and low-voltage cutoff.

Software

The plan is to keep the stock code running and add the AI as a separate service next to it, so the robot still works as a remote-controlled toy when the clever parts are broken.

Motion
Stock Arduino sketch from chillibasket/walle-replica. It queues animations, smooths servo acceleration and reads commands from the Pi without blocking. New: two distance sensors on the Arduino stop forward motion at an obstacle or a drop. The check runs on the Arduino, so it holds even when the Pi has crashed. Commands are single characters at 115200 baud: w a s d drive, q stops, j l tilt the head, i k move the eyes.
Remote control
New: a Bluetooth game controller is the main control. It pairs with the Pi, and a small service turns sticks and buttons into the commands above. No phone, browser or Wi-Fi is involved. If the connection drops, the service sends stop. Stock Flask web interface on the Pi, kept for setup and debugging: on-screen joystick, manual servo control, sounds, text to speech, a drag-and-drop block editor and a camera stream. A systemd service starts it at boot.
Face following
New. A face detector on the Pi turns the offset of the face from the image centre into head and eye commands. Candidates are MediaPipe and OpenCV.
Voice loop
New. Wake word or a push-to-talk button on the controller, speech to text, language model with tool calls, text to speech. Candidates are openWakeWord, a Whisper-family model and Piper. Which language model runs behind it is an open decision. The model never sends raw serial commands. It calls four named tools with limits on speed and duration, and the service translates those into the commands above.
Teach game
New. Image embeddings from a small pretrained vision model plus a nearest-neighbour classifier, all on the Pi. Her examples are stored as pictures in folders named after each object, so she can look at exactly what the robot learned from.

The stock code is licensed GPL-3.0. Everything I derive from it will be published under the same licence, in the project repository, which already holds the source of this site.

Safety and privacy

This is a robot with a lithium battery, a camera and a microphone in a house with a small child. These rules are part of the design.

  • Battery. The battery is a hard-cased tool pack, charged in its own dock outside the robot. A fuse and a low-voltage cutoff sit between the pack and everything else.
  • Walls and edges. Two distance sensors stop forward motion at an obstacle or a drop, whatever the controller, the Pi or the language model asked for.
  • Lost connection. When the game controller disconnects or its battery dies, the robot stops. The Arduino should also stop by itself when the Pi goes silent. That second check needs a heartbeat the stock code does not have, and it is not written yet.
  • Pinch points. Treads and arm linkages can catch small fingers. Drive speed is capped in software while she is the driver.
  • Printer and paint. The nozzle and bed are hot, so she watches and I remove the parts. Spray painting is an adult job, outdoors.
  • No pictures or audio of her leave the house. Face following and the teach game run on the robot. Speech is transcribed at home, and only the text goes to the language model.
  • The microphone has a physical off switch. Nothing is recorded before the wake word or the talk button.
  • Home network only. The stock web interface has a simple login that is not meant for untrusted networks, so the robot is never exposed to the internet.

Open decisions

  • Printer. Open-frame or enclosed. The enclosed one keeps hot and moving parts behind a door and costs about twice as much.
  • Language model. A cloud model answers faster and better. A local model on the laptop keeps even the text at home. I will measure response time for both before choosing.
  • Sharing the serial port. The stock web interface owns the connection to the Arduino. The AI service and the controller service should send their commands through that interface instead of opening the port a second time. How cleanly that works is untested.
  • Camera fit. Which CSI camera module fits behind the eye lens without changing the printed eye housing.
  • Battery fit. Whether the printed holder and a Bosch pack fit in the body, which was designed around a flat LiPo.
  • Sensor bracket. Where the two distance sensors mount on the front without spoiling the look, and whether the extra code still fits in the Arduino Uno's memory.
  • Wake word or talk button. A wake word has to cope with a child's voice. A talk button is simpler and only listens while it is held. Both are planned, and she decides which one she uses.
  • The robot's name. Not my decision.

    The main control is now a Bluetooth game controller paired with the Pi, so driving needs no phone, browser or Wi-Fi. The web interface stays for setup. One controller button becomes push-to-talk.     Tool battery, distance sensors, microphone

    Compared the plan with what is known about Microduck's hardware. Two things carried over: a hard-cased removable battery and distance sensing. The LiPo is replaced by a Bosch 12 V tool battery, and two distance sensors on the Arduino now stop the robot at walls and edges. The microphone is settled on a simple USB one with a physical mute. New estimate: CHF 940 to 1,100 with the printer.

  3. 15 Nov 2026

    Parts priced, Pi upgraded

    Priced every line of the parts list, mostly in Swiss shops. The estimate is CHF 920 to 1,100 with the printer and CHF 670 to 850 without. Switched the Pi from 4 GB to 8 GB after finding the Swiss price gap is about CHF 36, and moved speech to text onto the robot.

  4. 14 Nov 2026

    Plan published

    Chose a tracked robot over a walking one, dropped reinforcement learning from the main build, and split the AI into three layers she can each use on her own. Nothing is printed and nothing is ordered yet.
